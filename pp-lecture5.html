Chapter 5 · pp. 113–115

What is a gene?

Ask first: "Your body makes all its proteins from one set of instructions. Where are they kept?"

DNA: the recipe book

Packed into chromosomes. A long chain written in a 4-letter alphabet: A, T, G, C, twisted into a double helix.

Gene: one recipe

A stretch of DNA. Its letters spell out how to build one protein.

Protein: the dish

Building blocks folded into a shape: receptors (locks), transporters (pumps), enzymes.

We all have the same recipes, but with small spelling differences. The versions of the genes one person carries are called their genotype. One spelling difference can make a lock or pump work a little differently.

Why it matters: drugs act on proteins, and proteins come from genes, so a person's genotype can change how a drug works.

Chapter 5 · pp. 117, 128–129 Midterm

How can we find out what one gene does?

Think first: if you wanted to know what one part of a car does, what might you try?

Choose an experiment.

Knock-out mouse

A mouse bred with one specific gene switched off. Compare it with a normal mouse: whatever is different shows what that gene does.

Example (Leonard): mice without parts of the NMDA receptor (a glutamate lock) showed which proteins memory needs.

Transgenic mouse

A mouse given an extra gene, e.g. a human gene linked to Alzheimer's disease, so its brain makes the protein and we can study the disease.

Why mice?

Their genes are easy to change, they breed fast, and their behaviour is well studied.

But be careful

The gene is off from before birth, so a problem might come from how the brain developed, and other genes can take over the missing job.

Chapter 5 · pp. 117–118, 123

Depression runs in Ayesha's family. Is it genes?

Discuss: what else, apart from genes, do family members share?

Family studies

Compare how often relatives of patients have the illness with how often other people do.

17% vs 2%

relatives of people with panic disorder who also develop it, compared with people in general

A brother or sister with schizophrenia: 9–11 times the usual risk. With bipolar disorder: about 7 times.

The problem: families share genes and homes, food, stress, habits and ways of coping. A family study can't tell which one matters.

Why it matters: "it runs in the family" doesn't prove "it's in the genes". We need a cleverer method: twins.

Chapter 5 · pp. 118, 123–126 Midterm

What can twins tell us that families can't?

Identical twins

Share all their genes. (Also called monozygotic: from one egg.)

Non-identical twins

Share about half their genes, like ordinary siblings. (Dizygotic: from two eggs.)

Both kinds usually grow up in the same home. Concordance = how often, when one twin has the illness, the other has it too.

Guess, then click: do identical twins match more often?

The logic: same home, but identical twins share more genes. If identical twins match more often, the extra matching must come from genes.

Chapter 5 · pp. 118–119, 122 Midterm Updated

If an illness were caused only by genes, how often would identical twins match?

100%

They would always match, because they have the same genes.

In real life it's never 100%. For OCD, studies range from 25% to 87%. So environment and life events matter too.

Most mental illnesses are not caused by one faulty gene (as Huntington's disease is) but by many genes, each adding a little risk, together with life events.

Back to your guess on slide 1: how much of depression risk comes from genes?

genes 37%
other causes 63%

Depression: about 37% (twin studies pooled, 2000). For schizophrenia and bipolar disorder: 60–80% (Leonard).

Ayesha's answer: her children have a higher risk, not a certainty. Genes load the dice; life events still matter.

Chapter 5 · pp. 129–130 Midterm

Give two reasons the same dose could work differently in two sisters

1

How the body handles the drug

Liver enzymes break the drug down (Lecture 3). Different genes, faster or slower enzymes, so the blood level ends up too high or too low.

2

How well the drug acts on its target

The drug's target (e.g. the serotonin pump) is built from a gene. A different version can make the drug work better or worse.

Pharmacogenetics = the study of how genetic differences change people's response to drugs.

Why it matters: in a 5-mark answer on genes and drug response, these two doors are your explanation.

Chapter 5 · p. 130 Midterm Local data Lecture 3

Same dose, three people: who gets side effects, and who gets no benefit?

CYP2D6 GENOTYPE

Slow metaboliser: the drug builds up, into the side-effects zone.

Normal: the level settles in the zone where the drug works.

Ultra-fast metaboliser: cleared so fast the level never reaches the working zone.

Illustration of the idea, not real patient data.

CYP2D6

A liver enzyme that breaks down many antidepressants. Its gene comes in over 50 versions: some people have no working copy, some have extra copies.

Pakistan In 1,028 Pakistani volunteers, a related enzyme (CYP2C19) was slow in 6% and ultra-fast in 18%.

Riaz et al., 2019, Iranian Journal of Pharmaceutical Research

Chapter 5 · p. 131 Midterm

Could one gene predict whether an SSRI will work?

THE SYNAPSE

The serotonin transporter is the pump that takes serotonin back into the sending neuron. SSRIs work by blocking it. Its gene comes in a long and a short form.

Click a form to see what Leonard reports.

Long form

Makes more of the pump. Depressed patients with the long form respond better to SSRIs.

Short form

Weaker response to SSRIs. In bipolar disorder, an SSRI is more likely to trigger mania. May be linked to some side effects.

How it could guide treatment

Long form: an SSRI is a good first choice. Short form: consider a different kind of antidepressant, and watch closely for mania in bipolar disorder.

Chapter 5 · pp. 130–132 Midterm Updated

Is genetic testing ready for every patient?

Leonard's example: a gene variant predicts perfectly who won't respond to a drug. Sounds great?

But only 9 in every 100 patients carry it.

46 → 51

patients in 100 helped, even after testing everyone and avoiding the drug for those 9

Why the gain is so small

Each gene has only a small effect: a single test only about doubles the chance of predicting response. Response also depends on other genes, diet, other drugs and how the body handles the drug.

2023 guideline Tests for liver-enzyme genes (like CYP2D6) are now used to adjust doses. The evidence for the serotonin transporter gene is not strong enough to guide treatment.

Ayesha's answer: a test can help with the dose, but it can't yet pick her pill.

Exam-style question · 2.5 marks Write on your own · 5 minutes

Practise: write your answer

A genetic test shows that a patient has no working copy of the CYP2D6 gene. Explain how this result could guide her antidepressant treatment, and give one limitation of using genetic tests in this way.

What a full answer includes

  • What the result means: she is a slow metaboliser. CYP2D6 breaks down many antidepressants, so the drug will build up.
  • How it guides treatment: start on a lower dose, or choose a drug not broken down by CYP2D6, and watch for side effects.
  • One limitation: one gene has a small effect; response also depends on other genes, diet and other drugs, so the test can't predict everything.

Chapter 6 · pp. 133–134 Midterm Lecture 1

Where in the brain is serotonin made?

First found in blood

Named in 1947: "serum tonic", a substance in blood that tightens blood vessels. The same chemical, found in the gut, was called enteramine. Its chemical name is 5-HT.

Made in the raphé nuclei

In the brain, serotonin is made by a small cluster of cells in the brainstem called the raphé nuclei.

From there, fibres reach almost the whole brain, including the limbic system (emotion, alarm), and down the spinal cord.

Why it matters: one small group of cells shapes mood, anxiety, sleep and appetite.

Chapter 6 · pp. 134–138 Midterm Lecture 2

Serotonin has many receptors. Which one is the odd one out?

A receptor is a lock that a messenger fits into. Serotonin has 7 families of receptor, numbered 5-HT1 to 5-HT7.

Click each group.

A doorbell

Serotonin presses the bell; a G-protein passes the message on through a chain of steps inside the cell. Slower, but long-lasting.

A gate: the odd one out

5-HT3 is ionotropic: the receptor is a channel (a ligand-gated ion channel). Serotonin opens it and charged particles rush through. Very fast.

Buspironeacts on 5-HT1A: anxiety (Section 5)
Ondansetronblocks 5-HT3: sickness from chemotherapy
Sumatriptanacts on 5-HT1B/1D: migraine
LSDacts on 5-HT2A: hallucinations

Chapter 6 · pp. 147–149 Midterm

How does a serotonin neuron stop itself from overdoing it?

The raphé neuron fires at a steady rate and releases serotonin.

More serotonin around the neuron switches on its brake: firing slows down.

The brake: an autoreceptor

A serotonin sensor on the neuron itself (the 5-HT1A autoreceptor). When serotonin rises, it slows the neuron down, like a thermostat.

Serotonin and anxiety

Raphé fibres reach the limbic system, the brain's alarm. Leonard: in anxiety, some serotonin pathways that stir up the alarm are overactive.

Why it matters: both anxiety drugs on the next slide work through this brake.

Chapter 6 · pp. 147–149 Midterm

Why did Ayesha's panic get worse in week 1?

Guess first: an SSRI raises serotonin. Using the last slide, why might that make anxiety worse at first?

The SSRI blocks the pump, so more serotonin reaches the overactive alarm pathways: anxiety and panic can rise. The brake is pressed hard.

Serotonin reaching the alarm circuits

Anxiety

Over 2–4 weeks the brake and other receptors adapt (they become less sensitive). The system settles at a new balance: anxiety falls.

Serotonin reaching the alarm circuits
Strength of the brake
Anxiety

Buspirone presses the brake directly (it partly activates the 5-HT1A autoreceptor), so serotonin neurons fire less and anxiety falls. It doesn't act on GABA, unlike benzodiazepines.

Evidence: buspirone and similar drugs reduce anxiety in patients. In untreated panic patients, a serotonin receptor (5-HT2) on blood platelets is increased, and it returns to normal with effective treatment.

Applying it Midterm Lectures 3–4

What should Ayesha's doctor have told her before she started?

1

Warn her: anxiety and panic may get worse in the first week or two, because serotonin rises before the receptors adapt.

2

Start low, go slow: begin on a low dose and increase gradually.

3

Be patient: real benefit comes after 2–4 weeks. Don't stop suddenly.

4

Know the options: if an SSRI isn't tolerated, buspirone works on serotonin in a different way.

Ayesha's answer: the pill wasn't failing; her brain hadn't adapted yet. With a warning and a lower starting dose, she might have kept going.
