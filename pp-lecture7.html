What does mania look like?

Sort: click each to cycle ordinary happiness or energy → a sign of mania.

Leonard's six key features of mania

1 Very high or irritable mood2 Overactivity3 Fast speech that is hard to interrupt, and racing ideas4 Grand ideas about oneself5 Little need for sleep6 Easily distracted

Mania: a period of days or weeks with these features, strong enough to damage the person's life.

Bipolar disorder: episodes of mania that alternate with episodes of depression. Mania, bipolar disorder and depression together affect at least 2% of people.

Chapter 8 · pp. 193–194 Lecture 5

Does mania run in families?

Remember the twin method from Lecture 5: identical twins share all their genes; non-identical twins share about half.

Guess first: if one identical twin has manic-depression, how often does the other twin have it too? Hands up, then click the most popular answer.

Identical twins79%
Non-identical twins25%

Danish twin register: all same-sex twins born in Denmark, 1870–1920; 110 pairs with manic-depression (Leonard, p. 193).

Genes matter a lot: sharing all your genes makes it three times as likely that both twins are affected.

But it is never 100%: many genes are involved, each with a small effect, and life events matter too.

Chapter 8 · pp. 194–197 Lecture 6

What is happening in the brain in mania?

Mania is roughly the mirror image of depression: the messengers that excite the brain are too active, and GABA, the messenger that calms neurons, is too weak.

Three clues. For each one, ask: what does it suggest?

1

Drugs that boost dopamine (such as amphetamine) can trigger mania in people who are prone to it.

2

Drugs that block dopamine receptors calm mania.

3

Noradrenaline's waste product rises during mania and returns to normal after treatment.

Chapter 8 · pp. 197–199

How was lithium discovered?

Lithium is a simple salt, a close relative of the sodium in table salt. As a medicine it is given as lithium carbonate tablets.

1949: John Cade, Australia

The psychiatrist John Cade gave lithium to a small group of manic patients, almost by chance. They improved. Patients with other illnesses did not.

The first modern psychiatric drug

Lithium came before the first antipsychotic and the first antidepressants (Lecture 6). Leonard says it marks the beginning of psychopharmacology.

Think: lithium worked in 1949, but most countries didn't use it regularly until the early 1970s. What could explain a delay of 20 years?

It can poison. Too much lithium is dangerous, and at first there was no easy way to keep the dose safe.

Nobody could profit. A natural salt that "could be dug out of the soil" can't be owned by one company, so no company promoted it.

Chapter 8 · pp. 199–201, Table 8.1 Lecture 3 Updated

Why does lithium need regular blood tests?

Therapeutic window (Lecture 3): the range of blood levels where a drug works without poisoning. Lithium's window is narrow.

What happens as the level rises above the range?
1
Shaking hands that gets worse, vomiting and diarrhoea
2
Slurred speech, unsteady walking, sleepiness and confusion
3
Seizures and coma: a medical emergency

Why the level can rise: lithium leaves the body through the kidneys, which treat it like sodium. When the body is short of water or salt, the kidneys hold on to both, and lithium stays in.

Kinetic interaction Dehydration (heat, fasting, vomiting) and some diuretics ("water tablets") raise the lithium level.

Chapter 8 · pp. 201–204, Table 8.1

How does lithium work, and what are its side effects?

How it works

Inside neurons, lithium weakens the signals that the accelerator messengers switch on, and it strengthens the GABA brake.

It is slow: the full effect takes days to weeks.

It works in both directions: it treats mania and helps prevent both the highs and the lows.

Guess first: the body handles lithium like sodium. Which parts of the body might be affected?

Hands

A fine tremor (shaking) and muscle weakness.

Kidneys

Thirst and passing a lot of urine: the kidney stops responding to the hormone that saves water.

Thyroid

An underactive thyroid: lithium blocks the signal that tells the gland to work.

Weight

Weight gain, in up to 60% of patients.

Stomach

Nausea and diarrhoea, mostly when the level rises quickly after a dose.

Chapter 8 · pp. 204–208 Updated

Which other drugs calm mania?

Guess first: Hamza needs calming tonight. From the three clues on slide 4, what could a fast-acting drug do in the brain?

Antipsychotics: turn an accelerator down

Example: olanzapine. They block dopamine receptors and calm severe mania within hours to days.

Watch for: sleepiness and weight gain. The older drugs of this class can cause lasting movement problems, so the newer ones are preferred.

Anticonvulsant mood stabilisers: strengthen the brake

Example: valproate. An epilepsy drug that boosts GABA. Used when lithium doesn't work or isn't tolerated.

Watch for: stomach upset and interactions with other medicines (Lecture 3).

Updated · 2018 Valproate harms unborn babies: about 1 in 10 are born with a birth defect, and up to 4 in 10 have lasting problems with development. It is avoided in women and girls who could become pregnant.

Three classes of mood stabiliser: lithium · antipsychotics · anticonvulsants. One example of each: lithium carbonate, olanzapine, valproate.

Chapter 8 · pp. 208–209 Updated

Why do people keep taking medicine when they feel well?

Maintenance treatment: taking a medicine for months or years, while well, to prevent the next episode.

Bipolar disorder comes back. Without treatment most people have more episodes, and every episode can cost a job, money or relationships.

Updated · 2013 Lithium saves lives: in a review of 48 trials it cut the risk of suicide by more than 60% compared with placebo.

What if lithium alone isn't enough? Leonard: up to 50% respond poorly. Guess each step.
1
Start one mood stabiliser (usually lithium). Add an antidepressant only if depression needs it.
2
Switch to a different mood stabiliser (for example valproate).
3
Combine two mood stabilisers.
4
ECT, for severe episodes when drugs have failed (Lecture 6).

Hamza's answer: an antipsychotic to calm him now, and a mood stabiliser such as lithium, with blood tests, to keep him well without tipping him into depression.

When does anxiety become a disorder?

Normal anxiety is short and fits the danger: it helps you prepare for an exam.

An anxiety disorder is too strong, lasts too long, and stops normal life.

GABA is the brain's main calming messenger: it is used at about 40% of all synapses.

How do benzodiazepines calm anxiety so quickly?

Benzodiazepines are a class of calming drugs. Example: diazepam. They replaced the more dangerous barbiturates in the 1960s.

The GABA-A receptor (Lecture 2)

GABA opens a channel that lets chloride into the neuron. Chloride makes the neuron harder to fire.

A benzodiazepine helps GABA

It binds its own site and makes GABA work better: the channel opens more often. It does nothing without GABA. It works within an hour.

Barbiturates and alcohol act on the same receptor. This is why they add together.

One class, four uses: anxiety, sleep, seizures, muscle spasm. Some last for days (diazepam), others only hours: a half-life difference (Lecture 3).

Antidote: flumazenil blocks the benzodiazepine site and reverses an overdose.

Chapter 9 · pp. 234–236 Updated

Why are doctors careful with benzodiazepines?

Guess first: a drug that calms the whole brain within an hour sounds perfect. What could go wrong?

1

Sedation

Sleepiness, poor memory and poor coordination. In older people: falls and confusion.

2

Alcohol

Dynamic interaction Both act on GABA, so together they can dangerously slow breathing.

3

Tolerance and dependence

Tolerance: the brain adapts, so the same dose does less. Dependence: the person feels ill without the drug.

4

Rebound and withdrawal

On stopping, anxiety and sleeplessness come back worse; after high doses, seizures. 15–30% of people who take them for over a year struggle to stop.

Updated · 1988 What doctors do: use benzodiazepines for 2–4 weeks only, for severe anxiety, and reduce the dose slowly when stopping.

Sana's answer: the pill strengthens her GABA brake within an hour, but if she keeps taking it she risks tolerance, dependence and worse anxiety when she stops.

Chapter 9 · pp. 236–239 Lecture 5

What are the alternatives to benzodiazepines?

Guess first: a student has constant worry, must drive to university and has exams in two months. What should her medicine not do?

5-HT1A drugs: press the serotonin brake

Example: buspirone. It acts on the 5-HT1A receptor, the brake on serotonin neurons (Lecture 5).

Good: no sleepiness, no dependence, safe with alcohol.
Limits: takes up to 2 weeks to work; doesn't help panic attacks.

Beta-blockers: calm the body

Example: propranolol. It blocks noradrenaline and adrenaline at the heart and muscles, so the pounding heart and shaking stop.

Good: useful before a performance or a presentation.
Limits: it doesn't treat the worry itself.

For the student: a 5-HT1A drug fits. She stays alert, can drive and can study, and two months gives it time to work. A benzodiazepine would make her sleepy and harm her memory.

Chapter 9 · pp. 220–228 Lecture 6 Updated

Which drug class is the first choice for each anxiety disorder?

Guess before each row: benzodiazepine, 5-HT1A drug, beta-blocker or SSRI (a drug that keeps serotonin in the synapse for longer; example: fluoxetine)?

DisorderFirst choiceRemember
Panic disorderSSRIA benzodiazepine can be added for the first weeks only. Warn that anxiety may rise at first (Lecture 5).
Generalised anxiety disorderSSRI UpdatedLeonard (2003) said a benzodiazepine. A 5-HT1A drug is another option.
Social phobiaSSRITogether with psychological therapy.
Obsessive-compulsive disorderSSRIOften needs a higher dose and more time.
Post-traumatic stress disorderSSRITogether with psychological therapy.

The pattern: an SSRI is the first choice almost everywhere. A benzodiazepine is a short-term extra, not the main treatment.

Medicine is half the treatment: psychological therapy, such as CBT, works as well as drugs for many anxiety disorders.

The three cases: model points

GROUP 1

Hamza, 24

No sleep for four nights, reckless spending, non-stop talk, grand plans. Brought in by his family.

Mania (bipolar disorder): little sleep, fast speech, grand ideas, overactivity. An antipsychotic (olanzapine) to calm him within days, and lithium to prevent relapse. Lithium weakens the overactive signals and strengthens GABA; it takes days to weeks. Risks: lithium poisoning (blood tests, drink enough, care with diuretics); stopping when well (explain maintenance).

GROUP 2

Sana, 30

Sudden attacks of a pounding heart and fear of dying. Avoids crowded places. Takes a relative's sleeping pill.

Panic disorder with avoidance. An SSRI (fluoxetine) first: keeps serotonin in the synapse longer; takes weeks. Risks: anxiety may rise in the first weeks (warn her, start low); the borrowed benzodiazepine causes tolerance and dependence (2–4 weeks at most, reduce slowly, no alcohol).

GROUP 3

Mrs Rahman, 68

Worries constantly about everything, for over a year. Poor sleep. Her doctor wants to avoid falls.

Generalised anxiety disorder. An SSRI, or a 5-HT1A drug (buspirone): no sedation or dependence; takes 2 weeks or more. Risks: a benzodiazepine would cause falls and confusion at her age (avoid, especially long-acting ones); slow start may make her give up (explain the delay, review her).

Classroom exercise. Real treatment is decided by a doctor with the patient.
